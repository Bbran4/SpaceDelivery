A dungeon appears. Press it again. Different rooms. Different enemies. Different rewards. Different paths. It almost feels like the computer designed a new level for us. But what actually happened? How difficult is procedural generation? How does a computer decide what to create? What algorithms are doing the work? And with everything being called AI now... is procedural generation actually AI? Those questions sound technical. But I think the most interesting answers are actually about game design. Because procedural generation is really about designing possibilities. When I build a level by hand, I decide exactly where things go. The corridor goes here. The enemy waits there. The player gets a reward after this encounter. The boss is behind that door. I control the final experience directly. With procedural generation, some of that control moves into a system. Instead of designing one final level... I design rules capable of producing many possible levels. And that sounds simple. At the simplest level, it actually is. You can make a basic procedural generator surprisingly quickly. Choose random positions. Place random rooms. Pick random enemies. Scatter random rewards. Done. You have procedural generation. You also probably have a terrible game. The hard part begins when you want the generated result to consistently be worth playing. And that answers one of the most common questions. How difficult is procedural generation? Technically? It can be very easy. Good procedural generation? That can become extremely difficult. Generating ten decorative rocks is easy. Generating a playable dungeon is harder. Generating a dungeon with good pacing, meaningful exploration, balanced combat, interesting rewards, and enough variety to remain interesting after fifty runs... that is a serious design problem. The difficulty comes from all the relationships you need to protect. Let's imagine the worst dungeon generator possible. It creates rooms at random positions. Random sizes. Random connections. Then it randomly places enemies and loot. Every result is different. But randomness has no understanding of what makes a dungeon enjoyable. The boss might appear beside the entrance. Half the map might be unreachable. The player could receive their strongest weapon in the first room. Or they could go ten rooms without finding anything useful. The generator produced variety. But variety alone isn't enough. So we begin adding rules. Rooms cannot overlap. Important areas must remain connected. The boss needs to be far enough from the entrance. Certain rewards should appear before certain challenges. The player needs enough resources to reasonably survive. Maybe two extremely difficult encounters should not appear back to back. Every new rule removes outcomes we don't want. And this is the basic structure behind a lot of procedural generation. You begin with possibilities. Then rules, constraints, probabilities, and validation shape those possibilities into something useful. So how is procedural generation actually done? Usually through some combination of randomness, a seed, generation algorithms, design rules, constraints, and validation. The seed is especially useful. A seed is basically the starting value used by the random number generator. If the same generator uses the same seed under the same conditions, it can reproduce the same result. That means if a player tells me... Seed 48392 created an impossible dungeon... I can enter that same seed and reproduce what they saw. This is incredibly useful for testing. It also allows games to share generated worlds. Same seed. Same generated result. But the random number generator is only supplying variation. Something still has to decide how the world is built. That's where algorithms come in. And there isn't one procedural generation algorithm. There are many. Different algorithms are useful for different problems. For example, Binary Space Partitioning, usually called B-S-P, can repeatedly divide an area into smaller sections. You divide the space. Then divide it again. Place rooms inside those sections. Connect them. Now you have the beginnings of a dungeon. Another common technique is a random walk. Imagine putting a character on a grid and telling it to wander randomly. Everywhere it walks becomes floor. After enough steps, you can get irregular, cave-like spaces. Cellular automata can create organic environments in another way. You begin with a noisy grid. Then every cell changes based on the cells around it. After several iterations, the noise begins forming larger structures. For terrain, developers often use noise functions. Perlin noise. Simplex noise. And other forms of coherent noise. Pure randomness would make every point completely unrelated to the next. Noise creates smoother relationships between nearby values. That makes it useful for things like mountains, height maps, biomes, clouds, terrain, and resource distribution. Then there are systems like Wave Function Collapse. Wave Function Collapse works with possible tiles and rules about which tiles can exist beside each other. A road might need to connect to another road. A wall may require particular neighboring pieces. The system gradually reduces the available possibilities until an arrangement emerges. There are graph-based generators. Grammar systems. L-systems. Constraint solvers. Voronoi diagrams. Maze algorithms. And plenty of custom techniques developers create specifically for their own games. The algorithm matters. But from a game design perspective, the algorithm is only machinery. It doesn't know why your game is fun. You still have to give it that information. Suppose I use a perfect dungeon-generation algorithm. Every room is reachable. Nothing overlaps. The corridors look good. Technically, everything works. Then I fill every room by randomly selecting enemies. Twenty-five percent goblin. Twenty-five percent archer. Twenty-five percent tank. Twenty-five percent exploder. Now imagine the generator creates a tiny room with six ranged enemies and two tanks blocking the entrance. Congratulations. The layout works perfectly. The encounter is awful. Because enemy selection isn't only a probability problem. It's an encounter-design problem. A ranged enemy means something different in a large open room than it does in a narrow corridor. A tank changes how the player moves. An enemy that slows the player becomes much more dangerous when combined with something that punishes staying still. So the generator needs more information. Pressure. Space control. Movement. Range. Synergy. Difficulty. Now we're getting closer to procedural game design. Instead of asking... Which enemy should I randomly choose? we can ask... What kind of encounter should happen here? Then randomness chooses from options that fit that intention. The same problem appears with rewards. Imagine every room has a ten percent chance of containing health. Simple. But a health pickup means very little when the player already has full health. That exact same pickup can feel incredibly important after the player barely survives a difficult fight. Same item. Completely different experience. So perhaps the generator needs to remember what happened recently. How damaged is the player? When was the last reward? How difficult were the previous encounters? Has the player already received several powerful items? Now the procedural system is reacting to the run. It is helping shape pacing. And pacing is one of the biggest differences between a generator that creates content... and one that creates an experience. A handcrafted level usually has rhythm. Pressure. Relief. Discovery. Escalation. A quiet moment. Another challenge. If everything is independently random, that rhythm can disappear. You might get five intense encounters in a row. Or ten boring rooms. Or reward after reward until nothing feels valuable anymore. So procedural systems often use weighted probabilities. A fifty percent chance does not always need to remain fifty percent. Maybe after several difficult encounters, the chance of another difficult room decreases. Maybe the longer the player goes without seeing something rare, the more likely it becomes. Maybe a room type temporarily becomes unavailable after appearing. Now the generator has memory. And memory allows randomness to create rhythm. This leads to one of my favorite rules for procedural design. Protect what matters. Randomize what doesn't. If reaching the boss matters, protect connectivity. If the player needs a tool before a challenge, guarantee that some viable tool appears. If build variety matters, randomize which tool they receive. If exploration matters, randomize where they discover it. If pacing matters, control how extreme events are distributed. Some things should be guaranteed. Some should be likely. Some should be rare. And some combinations should never happen. The interesting design work is deciding which category each thing belongs to. Now let's answer another question. Is procedurally generated content considered AI? Usually, no. Procedural generation has existed in games for decades without needing machine learning or modern artificial intelligence. A dungeon generator using random numbers, B-S-P, noise, cellular automata, or Wave Function Collapse can run entirely from rules written by the developer. It doesn't need to learn from a dataset. It doesn't need a neural network. It doesn't need a large language model. The system follows algorithms and constraints. And that brings us to the related question. Is procedural generation the same thing as generative AI? No. They can overlap. But they describe different approaches. Traditional procedural generation usually means a developer defines rules, algorithms, probabilities, constraints, and relationships... then the computer produces variations inside that designed system. Generative AI usually involves a trained model producing new output based on patterns learned from training data. An image model generating a texture is generative AI. A language model writing dialogue is generative AI. A dungeon built using B-S-P and a random seed is procedural generation. Terrain created from Perlin noise is procedural generation. Of course, you can combine them. Maybe procedural rules decide where villages should exist... and a generative model creates some decorative content inside those villages. Or an AI system could help create parameters that are later passed into a procedural generator. The technologies can work together. But procedural generation itself does not automatically mean AI. And I think this distinction matters because procedural generation gives the designer a very specific kind of control. The generator is effectively encoding your design logic. Let's take a village. The simplest generator could choose twenty random buildings and scatter them across a field. House. House. Inn. Blacksmith. Shop. House. Different every time. But it probably won't feel like a real settlement. So we add relationships. The market prefers access to the main road. Homes tend to cluster. The blacksmith needs more open space. Wealthier buildings appear closer to certain landmarks. Paths form between locations people frequently visit. Now the generator isn't only placing objects. It's generating relationships between them. And those relationships begin telling a story. This is where procedural generation can become really interesting. Several simple rules interact. Something happens that the designer never explicitly scripted. But when you see the result... it makes sense. Maybe terrain creates a natural defensive position. The generator places a ranged enemy there. Another system spawns something that forces the player out of cover. Suddenly you have an encounter nobody deliberately authored. And yet every piece follows understandable rules. Those are some of the best moments in procedural games. The system surprises the player. Sometimes it even surprises the designer. But the result still feels like it belongs in the game. There is a danger on the other side too. Add too many rules and procedural generation becomes predictable. Every dungeon has the same pacing. Every village has the same structure. Every run gives you roughly the same sequence with different decoration. Eventually, the player starts seeing the template underneath everything. So procedural design is constantly negotiating between control and surprise. Too much freedom gives you nonsense. Too much control gives you repetition. Somewhere between those two is a space where the system can surprise you without breaking the game. And finding that space is probably the difficult part. This also makes procedural generation unusually difficult to test. You cannot generate one map, play it, and declare the system finished. You've tested one result. Seed 251 might be fantastic. Seed 252 might place the exit behind an inaccessible wall. Seed 253 might give the player three legendary weapons immediately. Seed 254 might create twenty minutes of repetitive rooms. Procedural generation has a wonderful habit of finding problems you were absolutely certain couldn't happen. So you start looking at patterns across many runs. What happens too often? What almost never happens? Where does difficulty spike? Where does the game become boring? Which combinations dominate? Which interesting situations almost never appear? Which seeds create impossible states? Sometimes you can automate part of this. Generate thousands of levels. Measure them. How long is the critical path? How many dead ends? How many rewards? How far is the boss from the entrance? How often does a particular room appear? Then humans still need to play the interesting cases. Because a mathematically valid dungeon can still feel terrible. So... how difficult is procedural generation? Writing a system that produces random things? Pretty easy. Writing one that produces valid things? Harder. Writing one that consistently produces interesting experiences? Much harder. And the more important procedural generation becomes to your game, the more time you usually spend designing the rules around it. Which is why I wouldn't begin by asking... How do I randomize this? I'd start somewhere else. What needs to remain true for this game to still feel like my game? Maybe every run needs meaningful build decisions. Maybe exploration should always contain uncertainty. Maybe combat needs periods of pressure and recovery. Maybe players should regularly discover combinations they haven't seen before. Maybe every generated settlement should still feel like people actually live there. Those ideas become constraints. The algorithms work underneath them. Randomness creates variation between them. And the player experiences the result. That is the part I find fascinating about procedural generation. You're designing something you may never see exactly the way the player sees it. You decide what must happen. What can happen. What should rarely happen. And what should never happen. Then you create a system capable of filling the space between those decisions. So procedural generation isn't really about asking a computer to design your game for you. You're still designing. You're just designing at a different level. Instead of creating one dungeon... you're creating the rules that define what a dungeon in your game is allowed to become. And when those rules are good enough... you can press one button... and get something you've never seen before... that somehow still feels like it belongs in your game.
